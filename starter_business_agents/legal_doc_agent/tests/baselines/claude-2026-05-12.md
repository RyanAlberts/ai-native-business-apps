## Key clauses to negotiate or red-flag

- **Residuals clause (Article 3):** This draft omits a residuals clause. AcmeCorp, as a larger company, may insist on language permitting their employees to use information "retained in unaided memory." This is founder-unfavorable — it can effectively nullify protection for orally disclosed ideas, architecture concepts, or product strategy. Resist or limit it narrowly.
- **Definition of Confidential Information (Article 1.1):** The 10-business-day window to confirm oral disclosures in writing is workable but creates administrative burden. Some founders shorten it to 5 days or require all sensitive oral disclosures to be marked on a log. Conversely, AcmeCorp may push to exclude oral disclosures entirely — push back if you expect sensitive discussions in meetings.
- **Term of confidentiality obligations (Article 4.2):** Two years from each disclosure is shorter than the typical 3–5 years used when trade secrets or proprietary source code is at stake. If you're sharing core IP, consider extending to 5 years or making trade secrets perpetual (subject to their status as trade secrets under applicable law).
- **Return/destruction mechanics (Article 5):** "Return or destroy" is standard for small deals. If you're sharing highly sensitive source code or data sets, consider requiring: (a) certified destruction only, (b) affidavit of destruction signed by an officer, and (c) specific reference to backup copies and cloud storage.
- **Governing law vs. AcmeCorp's home state (Article 8.1):** Delaware governing law is agreed here, but AcmeCorp is a New York corporation. They may push for New York law and venue. Delaware is reasonable for a Delaware LLC, but know that New York courts are also commonly used for commercial disputes, and New York has robust trade-secret precedent. Either is defensible; pick one and hold it.

## State-specific considerations

**Delaware (Founder LLC's state of formation):** Delaware courts, particularly the Court of Chancery, are highly experienced in commercial contract disputes and trade-secret matters. Delaware adopted the Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA) framework (federal) and has its own Uniform Trade Secrets Act (UTSA)-based statute. The choice of Delaware governing law here is well-supported and likely favorable for a Delaware-formed LLC. Delaware's covenant-against-assignment case law is also relatively founder-friendly.

**New York (AcmeCorp's state of incorporation):** New York has strong commercial contract courts and has adopted the DTSA at the federal level. New York does *not* follow the Uniform Trade Secrets Act — it relies instead on common law trade-secret doctrine, which can make the definition of "trade secret" narrower or harder to enforce. If either Party anticipates disclosing information that is borderline in its protectable status, the Delaware choice here reduces that risk. If AcmeCorp insists on New York law during negotiations, flag this distinction to your attorney.

## Common founder mistakes

- **Signing a mutual NDA when only outbound protection is needed.** If you're only sharing your IP with AcmeCorp and receiving nothing sensitive in return, a unilateral (one-way) NDA is simpler, gives you more control, and avoids inadvertently obligating you to protect AcmeCorp's information. Use mutual only when the information flow is genuinely bidirectional.
- **Failing to mark or log oral disclosures.** If this draft is used, oral disclosures must be confirmed in writing within 10 business days or they may not be protected. Founders routinely disclose sensitive roadmap details in demo calls and forget to follow up — create a simple email template to send after every significant discussion.
- **Not checking signatory authority.** Ensure the person signing for AcmeCorp has actual authority to bind the corporation (e.g., an officer or someone with a board resolution). A signature from a non-authorized employee may render the agreement unenforceable.
- **Treating an NDA as a substitute for proper IP documentation.** An NDA protects confidential information you *share*; it does not establish ownership of IP created *during* the relationship. If Founder LLC and AcmeCorp collaborate on building anything, a separate IP assignment or work-made-for-hire agreement is essential to prevent ownership disputes later.

## Key clauses to negotiate or red-flag

- **🚩 Cookie Consent (EU/ePrivacy):** This policy assumes you either use only strictly necessary cookies or have implemented a separate consent banner. If you use any non-essential cookies (Google Analytics, Intercom tracking, etc.) for EU users, you are likely required to obtain prior opt-in consent under the ePrivacy Directive *before* setting those cookies. This is separate from GDPR and is routinely overlooked by SaaS founders. Have your attorney confirm your cookie stack.

- **🚩 Legitimate Interests Balancing Test:** Using "Legitimate Interests" as a lawful basis (especially for analytics) requires you to document a Legitimate Interests Assessment (LIA) showing your interests aren't overridden by users' rights. This is an internal document but is required under GDPR — your attorney should help you prepare it before you go live with EU users.

- **🚩 EU Representative Requirement:** If you process EU residents' data at scale (even as a small SaaS), GDPR Art. 27 may require you to appoint a local EU representative. There is no fixed threshold; it turns on the nature and volume of processing. Many early-stage US SaaS companies skip this; it's a real enforcement risk. Ask your attorney.

- **🚩 Sub-Processor List and DPAs:** You must have signed Data Processing Agreements with every sub-processor touching EU personal data (Stripe, your cloud host, email provider, analytics tool). Stripe offers a DPA at stripe.com/legal/dpa. Confirm the others. GDPR Art. 28 requires this.

- **🚩 Retention Periods:** The retention periods above are reasonable defaults, but you must actually implement them operationally (automated deletion jobs, data maps). A policy that says "we delete in 3 years" but your database retains data indefinitely creates legal exposure. Confirm your engineering team can honor these periods.

## State-specific considerations

**Delaware (formation state):** Delaware does not currently have a comprehensive consumer privacy statute, so your primary U.S. privacy obligations flow from other states where your *users* reside (California, Virginia, Colorado, Texas, Connecticut, etc.) rather than your formation state. Delaware governing law is appropriate for your Terms of Service and contracts, but privacy obligations are generally determined by the location of your *customers*, not your incorporation. That said, this is a rapidly evolving area — Delaware passed the Delaware Personal Data Privacy Act (DPDPA) effective January 1, 2025, so verify whether your processing activities trigger its thresholds (35,000+ consumers/year or 10,000+ consumers if 20%+ revenue from data sales — the latter likely doesn't apply since you don't sell data).

**California (CCPA/CPRA — high likelihood of applicability):** Even though you're Delaware-based, if you have California users and meet any of CPRA's thresholds (annual gross revenue > $25M; buy/sell/share data of 100,000+ consumers; or derive 50%+ revenue from selling/sharing personal data), you are subject to CCPA/CPRA. At early stage you may be below threshold now, but build in the infrastructure (opt-out mechanism, request handling, 45-day response SLA) so you're not scrambling when you scale. The section in this policy provides baseline compliance. If you run targeted advertising, add a "Do Not Share" opt-out link and update the CPRA disclosure.

## Common founder mistakes

- **Publishing without a cookie audit first.** Most SaaS apps drop a dozen third-party cookies on load (analytics, support widgets, heatmaps). Publishing a policy that says "we only use functional cookies" when your site actually runs Google Analytics or Intercom is an immediate compliance gap — and EU regulators prioritize cookie enforcement.

- **Forgetting to actually execute sub-processor DPAs.** Founders assume that checking a vendor's "GDPR compliant" box is enough. It isn't. You need a signed DPA with each processor touching EU data. Stripe, AWS, Sendgrid, Intercom — all of them. Most offer self-serve DPAs; many founders never execute them.

- **Treating the Privacy Policy as a "set it and forget it" document.** Every time you add a new tool (a new analytics platform, a CRM, a session-replay tool), you need to (a) sign a DPA, (b) update your sub-processor list, (c) confirm it doesn't trigger new consent requirements. Build a lightweight review trigger into your vendor onboarding checklist.

- **Conflating "we don't sell data" with CCPA exemption.** CCPA's definition of "share" is broad and includes sharing for cross-context behavioral advertising (e.g., running Facebook Pixel, Google Ads remarketing). Even if you don't *sell* data in the traditional sense, if you run these ad tools, you may be "sharing" under CCPA and need the opt-out mechanism.

## Key clauses to negotiate or red-flag

- **Definition of "Cause" (Section 3.1(b)).** This is the single highest-friction clause in cofounder splits. The list above is deliberately narrow to protect the terminated founder. The remaining founder may push for a broader definition (e.g., "persistent underperformance"). Your attorney should align this definition with your state's employment law standards and any investor term sheet requirements, which often mandate a specific Cause definition.

- **Acceleration scope — single vs. double trigger (Sections 3.1 and 3.4).** Full 100% single-trigger acceleration on involuntary termination is aggressive but founder-protective. Many VCs will ask you to eliminate or reduce this in a Series A term sheet. Consider negotiating *partial* acceleration (e.g., 50% of unvested shares) as a compromise that is more investor-friendly while still providing meaningful protection.

- **50/50 deadlock risk.** A 50/50 equity split creates structural deadlock risk when the Founders disagree on a material business decision. This Agreement includes arbitration, but you should also discuss with your attorney whether to add a casting-vote mechanism (e.g., CEO has tiebreaker on operational decisions), a mandatory buyout provision, or a shotgun clause as a last resort.

- **Right of First Refusal vs. Co-Sale Right.** This draft includes a ROFR but not a co-sale (tag-along) right. When investors come in, they will almost certainly require both in a separate Investors' Rights Agreement. Make sure your attorney aligns this Agreement's transfer restrictions with future investor agreements to avoid conflicts.

- **83(b) Election deadline.** This is not a negotiation issue — it is a hard deadline. Each founder must file an 83(b) election with the IRS **within 30 calendar days** of the share issuance date. Missing this window can result in ordinary income tax on the value of shares as they vest (potentially very costly if the company appreciates). This is arguably the most time-critical action item in this entire document.

## State-specific considerations

**Delaware (assumed default):** Delaware corporate law is well-developed and familiar to investors, attorneys, and courts. The DGCL provides clear rules on share issuance, board authority, and stockholder rights. One nuance: under Delaware law, the Board of Directors (not the Founders themselves) technically has authority over equity issuances. If the Company does not yet have a Board, ensure that the Founders, as sole directors, formally adopt a Board resolution authorizing the share issuance concurrent with this Agreement.

**California operations:** If either Founder lives or works primarily in California, California labor law applies to the employment relationship regardless of the entity's state of formation. California's broad-form non-compete law (Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600) renders most non-compete clauses unenforceable — which is why this Agreement does not include one. Additionally, California has specific rules around IP assignment: Labor Code § 2870 provides that an employee cannot be required to assign IP developed entirely on their own time without use of company resources. Founders should be aware of this carve-out in Section 6.1. If operating in California, New York, or Washington, have a local employment attorney review Article 4, 6, and 7.

## Common founder mistakes

- **Skipping the 83(b) election.** This is the most common and most costly mistake. Without it, each monthly vest event is a taxable event at the *then-current* fair market value of the shares. File within 30 days of share issuance — no exceptions, no extensions.
- **Relying on a handshake equity split.** Many founding teams operate for months or years on a verbal agreement ("we're 50/50"). Without a signed, written document with vesting attached, a departing founder may claim 50% of the company even if they left on day 60. This Agreement is your protection.
- **Not assigning pre-incorporation IP.** If one or both founders built prototypes, wrote code, or created designs *before* the company was formed, that IP may not automatically belong to the company. Exhibit A and Section 6.1 address this — make sure the prior inventions disclosure is complete and accurate.
- **Assuming this Agreement covers everything.** A Cofounder Agreement works alongside — but does not replace — a Restricted Stock Purchase Agreement, an IP Assignment Agreement, a Confidentiality Agreement, and eventually a Stockholders' Agreement when investors come in. Think of this document as the term sheet for the founder relationship; the supporting documents fill in the legal mechanics.
