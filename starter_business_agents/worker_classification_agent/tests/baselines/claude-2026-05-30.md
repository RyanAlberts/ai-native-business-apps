## State Framework: California AB5 (ABC Test)

**Framework:** California Labor Code §2775 (AB5) — *stricter than any federal test.* Under AB5, a worker is **presumed an employee** unless the hiring entity proves **all three** of the following prongs:

| Prong | Requirement | Satisfied? | Analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Worker is free from the company's control and direction in performing the work | ❌ **FAILS** | Fixed M–F 9–5 schedule, full-time hours, company-issued MacBook, and a lead engineer role at a software company all indicate significant control over how the work is done. |
| **B** | Work is performed outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business | ❌ **FAILS** | You are a software startup. This person is your lead software engineer. Writing your software is *literally* your core business. This prong is almost never satisfiable in this context. |
| **C** | Worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade or business of the same nature | ❌ **FAILS** | The worker has no other clients. There is no evidence of an independently established engineering business. |

**→ Fails A, B, and C. Under AB5, the worker is a W-2 employee as a matter of California law. The federal analysis below is instructive but does not change this outcome.**

## Cost of Misclassification

Since you're proposing 1099 but the answer is definitively W-2, the downside exposure is substantial:

- **Back payroll taxes:** 7.65% employer FICA share × all wages paid × IRS lookback period (typically 3 years, up to 6 for substantial understatement). At $150/hr × 40hrs × 52 weeks, that's ~$312K/year in wages — roughly **$23,900/year in employer FICA alone**, before penalties and interest.
- **California EDD exposure:** The EDD can assess back UI and SDI contributions plus a 10% penalty for failure to withhold. California's audit lookback can extend to 8 years in fraud cases.
- **California Labor Code §226.8 penalties:** Willful misclassification carries civil penalties of **$5,000–$25,000 per violation**, plus a public notice requirement. The "we wanted to avoid payroll setup" rationale could be characterized as willful.
- **Worker claims:** A misclassified worker in California can sue for: unpaid overtime (CA requires daily OT over 8 hrs), missed meal and rest break premiums, unreimbursed business expenses (Labor Code §2802), and potentially waiting-time penalties if not paid correctly upon termination. These suits regularly result in 6-figure settlements even for a single worker.

## Common Founder Mistakes — Specific to This Situation

- **"We have an IC agreement, so we're covered."** Not true. California courts and the EDD look at the actual working relationship. A contract that says "contractor" while the reality is M–F 9–5 on a company MacBook is not a defense — it can actually evidence willfulness.
- **"They want to be 1099 for tax reasons."** The worker's preference has zero legal weight in California. AB5 is a worker *protection* law — a worker cannot waive it, and neither can you.
- **"Paying $150/hr makes them a high earner, not an employee."** Compensation level is irrelevant to classification. California has no "highly compensated" exemption from AB5 (unlike the federal FLSA white-collar exemption, which is a different analysis).
- **"We'll deal with it when we're bigger."** The risk accrues from day one. A disgruntled worker — or a competitor's attorney — can trigger an EDD audit at any time. Retroactive reclassification 2–3 years in is far more expensive than proper payroll setup today.
- **"This is fine for now because we're offering no benefits."** Withholding benefits from a misclassified worker doesn't make them a contractor — it just adds benefit-value claims to the worker's potential lawsuit.

## State Framework: Massachusetts ABC Test (M.G.L. c.149 §148B)

**Framework:** Massachusetts ABC test — *among the strictest in the US, predating California's AB5.*
**Rule:** Fail **any one** of A, B, or C → worker is a **W-2 employee**, full stop.

To classify the worker as a 1099 independent contractor, **you** (the hiring company) must prove all three prongs:

| Prong | What it requires | Analysis | Pass / Fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** — Freedom from control | Worker is free from your control and direction in *fact* and under contract | He brings his own crew, truck, tools; sets his own framing methods; you direct *results* (a framed house), not *process* (how he swings the hammer). Likely satisfied. | ✅ **PASS** |

This prong almost certainly **fails**. | 🔴 **LIKELY FAIL** |
| **C** — Independent trade/business | Worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade of the same nature | He has 4 other GC clients, his own crew, truck, and tools — he operates as a standalone framing business. Clearly satisfied. | ✅ **PASS** |

**Bottom line on the ABC test:** Prong B is likely failing. Under M.G.L. c.149 §148B, one failure = W-2. The strength of Prongs A and C does not save the classification.
